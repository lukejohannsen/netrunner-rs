## 7. A public server: lobbies by format, decks nobody sees — OPEN (26 September 2026)

Asked for as: a server that simply exists on the internet, where people join a lobby for a format and are matched against a random opponent. Each player brings their own deck, and neither the opponent nor anyone browsing learns what it is or what it is called. Rated play is for this server; a game hosted peer to peer stays casual. Tournaments may come later.

**Decided with the person, 26 September 2026:**
- **A lobby per format, on one server.** It is not one daemon per format.
- **No picking an opponent.** A lobby pairs whoever is waiting. Listing the waiting players and challenging one was proposed and declined. A named room remains the way two people who know each other meet.
- **No dealt decks where the game is about the decks.** The player's surprise is the point, so the host dealing a deck to someone who brought none is not wanted there. Everyone has the built-in decks to bring.
- **Connecting asks for no deck; looking for a game does** (later the same day). A client attaches to a server with nothing but a name. It browses lobbies, which are open or closed by id and an optional password, and joins one — all without a deck. A deck is asked for only when the player looks for a game. Choosing a chair brings that side's deck; choosing a random chair brings one deck of each side. A player picks afresh for every game from all the decks their client holds, without reconnecting, and the same holds on a peer connection.
- **Lobbies are the server's and the players'.** The server has one open lobby per format it offers, and it never goes away. Any player may make a lobby, open or closed, in a format the server offers; a player's lobby goes when its last player leaves.

**Stages, each its own branch:**

1. **Lobbies by format** (`feat/format-lobbies`, built). `ServeOptions::formats` lists the lobbies, every format by default with Startup first; `netrunner_server --format startup,standard` narrows them. `Connect::format` names the lobby, and `None`, which is what an older client sends, is the first one. A format the daemon does not offer is refused, naming the ones it does. A player is paired only within their format and room, and a brought deck is checked against the lobby's format. `MatchList::lobbies` counts each lobby's public waiters, and `MatchSummary::format` names a match's lobby. A game hosted from a client's menu offers only the host's chosen format. The desktop's Host and Join pages choose the format as pills and offer the decks legal in it. The terminal joins its Settings format and names it in the form's title. Tests: two players in different formats wait apart, a Connect naming no format joins the first lobby, the list reports lobbies and a match's format, and an unoffered format is refused.
2. **A brought deck is private** (`feat/private-decks`, built). A saved deck's id is a slug of the name the player gave it, and it used to be sent to the opponent in `MatchJoined` and to anyone listing in `MatchSummary`. The server must see the whole list to validate it and run it; nobody else sees any of it but what the rules reveal. So:
   - Each seat is told only its own deck's id; the other side's is always empty. `SeatTicket::joined` is the one place the message is built, for the first seating and every resume.
   - `MatchSummary` names no decks at all, and gives the lobby instead. Hiding only brought decks while still naming dealt ones was rejected: a dealt list is still a list the opponent should not know, and a spectator is one message from telling them.
   - The identities stay public, because the rules show them.

   Tests: each seat, and a resume, is told its own deck and never the other's, and the list names neither. The rotation and pinning tests now read the identities off the views.
3. **A server deals nobody a deck** (`feat/no-dealt-decks`, built). `ServeOptions::deals` is off by default: a `Connect` with no deck is refused at the door, pointing to the built-in decks. `netrunner_server --deal` turns dealing back on, for a daemon that wants the old behaviour and for the tests of dealing itself. A bot seat is still dealt its own deck.
   - **Both clients no longer offer a deal.** `online::deck_choices` lists every deck legal in the format, built-in first then saved, Corp decks before Runner. `DeckChoice` and its three "let the host deal" entries are gone.
   - **The terminal's `--server` path always brings a deck:** `--deck`, or `--corp-deck`/`--runner-deck` when only `--side` is given. With neither it stops before connecting and says why.
   - A game hosted from a client's menu deals nothing either, so the host and the joiner each bring a deck, and two decks for the same side do not pair.

   Tests:
   - `a_server_deals_nobody_a_deck_by_default`: refused at the door, never queued, and a brought deck still queues.
   - The existing tests of dealing opt in with `deals: true`.
   - The desktop's and the terminal's host-and-join tests now bring a deck for each side.

6. **Tournaments, run as Null Signal Games runs them in person.** The design below is grounded in their Organized Play Policies (v1.6.2, October 2023; read 26 September 2026, section numbers theirs). **Stage (a), registration and deck commitments, is built** (`feat/tournament-registration`, 8 October 2026 — the entry follows the design), **stage (b), the pages in both clients and single-sided Swiss** (`feat/tournament-pages` and `feat/tournament-swiss`, 9 October 2026), **and stage (c), the seed commit-reveal** (`feat/tournament-seed-commit-reveal`, 9 October 2026); the cut is next.

   **What their events do:**
   - **Structure.** Swiss rounds, then a cut (1.1.1–1.1.2). *Double-sided* Swiss plays both sides against the same opponent in a 65–70 minute round (1.1.5.1). *Single-sided* Swiss plays one game in 40 minutes, with the software choosing and balancing the sides (1.1.5.2); the World Championship runs eleven rounds of it. The cut takes the top 3–16 players (1.1.6), always single-sided (1.1.7), and double elimination except at casual events (1.1.9–1.1.10). The first cut round's higher seed picks sides; after that each player plays the side they have played less (1.1.11). Appendix II gives rounds and cut size by attendance.
   - **Scoring.** Per game: a win 3, a tie 1, a loss 0 (1.1.4). A bye scores a full round's win, goes to a random player in round 1 and afterwards to the lowest-scoring player who has not had one (1.1.3). The tiebreakers are Strength of Schedule, then Extended SoS, then random (1.1.6.2–1.1.6.4). A tied elimination game goes to the higher seed (1.1.12).
   - **Time.** When time is called, the player whose turn it is finishes it and the other takes one more turn. Then more agenda points wins, otherwise it is a tie (1.1.5.3). If time is called during the first game of a double-sided round, the second game is a tie (1.1.5.4).
   - **Decks.** At a competitive event decklists are required (1.3.2) and submitted before round 1 (2.1.4). The list is the truth over the physical deck (2.1.6), and a mismatch or an illegal list costs a game (4.4). Lists are private during Swiss. For the cut they are handed to the opponent to read for 3–5 minutes and taken back (1.1.8, 2.4.3). The World Championship takes them from NetrunnerDB private lists.
   - **Online.** A disconnected player has 5 minutes to reconnect, or loses the game (1.5.6). An asynchronous round is scheduled within 72 hours (1.5.5). A *sanctioned* online event must be played on jinteki.net (1.5.2), so events on this server would be unsanctioned community events.
   - **Conduct.** An intentional draw scores a tie, and in double-sided Swiss a "241" lets the first game decide the round. Both must be agreed with a judge within 5 minutes of the round starting (2.5.8–2.5.9). Outside assistance and collusion lead to disqualification (4.3).

   **What an engine removes, and what it does not.** Most of their penalty chapter is about human error that the engine makes impossible: missed triggers, illegal actions, overdraws, revealed cards, illegal board states, marked cards and deck-versus-list mismatches (4.4–4.8, 5). The server also plays from the registered list itself, so there is no physical deck to disagree with it. What remains is a question of trust — in the players, and in the server — and that is what the design answers.

   **The design:**
   - **Registration locks a pair of decks, one Corp and one Runner, for the whole event.** Both are submitted before round 1, validated against the event's format and held by the server, which deals every game from the registered list. Nothing is submitted per round, so there is nothing to swap mid-event. A commitment is published beside each registration: `SHA-256(salt ‖ canonical deck bytes)`, signed with the player's key (§5).
     - *Canonical* means sorted card ids and counts, never re-serialized JSON — §5's rule for signed statements.
     - The salt is random and kept by the player and the server. Without it, a hash of a well-known decklist can be confirmed by anyone who guesses the list and hashes it, and the commitment would leak exactly what the privacy stage protects.
     - The commitment is the player's receipt that the server ran their deck unaltered. At the end of the event, or at the cut, the list and salt are revealed and anyone can check them against it.
   - **Deck lists private through Swiss.** Opponents see only what the rules reveal (stage 2). At the cut each player is shown their opponent's list for a few minutes before the game, as at a table (1.1.8). The server records that it did.
   - **The shuffle can be checked.** The seed is a commit-reveal between the server and both players. The server commits to `H(server_secret)` when the pairing is posted; each player's seat commitment carries a nonce of their own; the seed is `H(server_secret ‖ nonce_corp ‖ nonce_runner)`, and the secret is revealed with the result. So the server cannot pick a favourable shuffle, and a player can replay their game from the record — which replays bit-identically today — to check every draw.
   - **Results are receipts, standings a fold.** Each game ends in a server-signed receipt (§5 stage c) naming the event, round, both keys, both deck commitments, the seed commitment and the result. Standings, pairings and the SoS and ESoS tiebreakers are a deterministic function of the receipts log and the published seeding. Anyone holding the log can recompute them, and a disputed standing is a recomputation.
   - **Identity is the key** (§5): a registration names a key, not a name, and a player may rename themselves mid-event.
   - **What still needs a person:** outside assistance, collusion and intentional draws. The protocol gives the organizer tools — an intentional draw or 241 offered by both players inside the first 5 minutes, and a drop — and cannot detect coaching. A tournament organizer role, signed by its own key, has the final say, as the policies give the organizer (3.2.1).
   - **Time is the clock the server already has**, set to the round length. The end-of-round rule (1.1.5.3) is a server rule: the current turn and one more, then agenda points. The reconnect grace is 5 minutes (1.5.6).

   Staging when it is built: registration and deck commitments; single-sided Swiss with the side balancing, byes and tiebreakers; the seed commit-reveal; the cut with its side-selection rules and decklist viewing; then double-sided Swiss and asynchronous rounds.

   **Stage (a): registration and deck commitments** (`feat/tournament-registration`, 8 October 2026). The server and the client core, as stage 4a was before the clients' pages (4b–4d): a tournament is made, listed, entered and left over the attached connection, and nothing is drawn for it yet — both clients' Online screens ignore the three events with a comment saying so, and the pages are the next sub-stage's first job.
   - **A tournament is a proved key's, on a daemon that keeps things.** `ClientMessage::CreateTournament { name, format }` is refused on a connection that proved no key — the key is the organizer, the person the policies give the final say (3.2.1) — and on a daemon with no `--data-dir`, because a tournament outlives any socket. It gets a six-character code as a player's lobby does, is answered with `ServerMessage::Tournament { tournament }` (a `TournamentInfo`: id, name, format, organizer, `TournamentState::Registering`, entrants in registration order), and is kept whole in `tournaments.json`, rewritten on every change like the players file, so a daemon restarted mid-registration holds the lists it promised to deal from. `TournamentState` has one variant, so the rounds' stages add theirs under an exhaustive match. `ListTournaments` answers `Tournaments`, oldest first.
   - **A registration locks two decks behind a signed commitment.** `Register { tournament, corp, runner, salt, statement }`: the statement is a `statements::RegistrationStatement` — the tournament, the server's key, the player's key and each deck's `deck_hash` under the salt — signed by the player under `REGISTRATION_TAG`. The server refuses it, each with its reason and nothing kept, when it is signed by another key, does not verify, names another tournament, key or server, carries a salt outside 1–64 characters, puts a deck in the wrong chair, has a deck the tournament's format refuses, or names hashes that are not the decks sent; then keeps the decks, the salt and the statement, replacing the key's earlier entry while registration is open, and answers `Tournament`. **What the wire carries is the commitment, never the list** (`Entrant { name, key, corp_hash, runner_hash, commitment }`): decklists are private through Swiss (1.1.8), and a tournament's JSON names no deck. `Unregister { tournament }` withdraws while registration is open. The tournament's code is read as the code it spells, in the request and in the statement alike, because a code is read out and typed back in any case.
   - **The salt is the player's half.** The client machine signs the statement (`connection::Connection::register`, which returns it) with the key it proved and the server key it was challenged with; the driver makes the salt — the machine has no randomness of its own — and keeps `identity::Registration { statement, salt, corp, runner }` as a line of `registrations.jsonl` beside the key (`remote::Attached::register`), so the player can check the list the server reveals at the cut, or show their own. The driver's command channel grew a second kind of command for it, since a registration is not a `ClientMessage` until the machine has signed it; a client with no key is told so without a round trip.
   - Tests: the server's over real sockets (`tests/tournament.rs`: a stateless daemon and an unproved connection refused; every refusal above and the taken registration's commitment verifying with the hashes of the decks sent and no deck id on the wire; a replaced entry; a withdrawal; a second daemon on the same data directory holding the same tournament and entrant); the machine's (the statement signed for this server with the hashes of the two decks, and a connection with no key signing nothing); the driver's (a tournament made, entered and left at a real daemon, the salt kept with the statement, a keyless client refused at home).
   - **Left for stage (b):** the pages in both clients, the organizer closing registration, and the Swiss itself.

   **Stage (b), first half: the pages in both clients** (`feat/tournament-pages`, 9 October 2026). A tournament is now reached from the Server page of either client — the terminal's `Tournaments…` row, the desktop's button beside Make a lobby — and the three events stage (a) left ignored are drawn.
   - **The words are one module** (`netrunner_client::tournament`): a tournament's line for a list ("Friday · Startup · 1 entered · taking registrations"), its state's phrase (exhaustive over `TournamentState`, so the rounds' stages add theirs), an entrant's line (the name they attached with and their key's fingerprint, since a name is a label anyone may wear), this key's entry and whether it holds the tournament, and the line over the buttons that says which ("You hold this tournament. Not entered yet."). **The code is the page's heading, not the row's:** it is what a person reads out to a friend, not what they scan a list by.
   - **Three pages under the Server page, in both clients:** the list (asked for afresh each time it opens, because a tournament moves while nobody is looking; "asking…", "this server holds none", or the rows), a tournament's page (code, format, state, who holds it, the standing line, the entrants, and — only for a key, only while registration is open — the two decks to lock in from those legal in *its* format and Register, with Withdraw for an entrant and "Register again with these decks" replacing an entry), and Hold a tournament (a name and a format, the lobby's by default). Escape walks back a page at a time, attached still; the desktop's `Intent::Back` and the terminal's modes carry the `ServerPage` under them so nothing is re-fetched on the way back.
   - **Whose key the page speaks for is the server's word, not the file's**: `MyStanding`'s answer already echoed the key the connection proved, so the terminal's `ServerPage::key` and the desktop's `ServerState::key` read it off `Standing { key, .. }` (the desktop's intent grew the field) rather than opening `identity.key` a second time — a client with no key is told it cannot hold or enter, and offers neither button. The decks on a tournament's page are its own list (`tournament_decks`, `TournamentDecks(format)`), never the lobby's: the formats differ, and a deck chosen for a game is not a deck locked for an event. On the desktop the same deck drop-down markers serve both, and `Intent::SetCorpDeck` reads the page.
   - **What the client keeps is unchanged**: the driver signs the statement and keeps the salt (stage a); the pages only choose the decks, and the tests read the registration the driver kept back to check the decks the page chose are the ones committed.
   - Tests: the desktop model's (opening lists, a row asks for its format's decks, Register sends the two chosen and says which side has none, the server's answer brings the page level, Withdraw only for an entrant, Back walks the pages; a keyless client and a closed tournament offer no entry; the form holds one and its page opens), the desktop's headless Online test and the terminal's screen test, both over a real daemon with a data directory (one client holds, another opens, registers with the decks shown, is drawn as entered, withdraws; the organizer's refresh sees both; the terminal's keyless screen is refused Hold and drawn with no entry rows), and the words module's own.
   - **Left for stage (b)'s second half:** the organizer closing registration and beginning a round, single-sided Swiss with the side balancing, byes and tiebreakers, and a seat at one's table.

   **Stage (b), second half: single-sided Swiss** (`feat/tournament-swiss`, 9 October 2026). The organizer begins the rounds, the paired sit at their tables, and the standings fold off the rounds on either end.
   - **The Swiss is its own engine-free crate, `netrunner_tournament`**, as `netrunner_rating` is: the scoring (3/1/0, a bye a win: 1.1.3–1.1.4), the standings with Strength of Schedule and Extended Strength of Schedule as Cobra computes them (the mean over opponents of points per round played, byes rounds and never opponents; the mean of the opponents' SoS), and the next round's pairing — the bye to the lowest without one, neighbours in the standings paired with a search that avoids a rematch wherever any pairing without one exists, and sides to whoever has played that side less, the lower-standing taking the Corp when even. **The seeding is the randomness:** every "random" the policies ask for (round 1's order and bye, the last tiebreak) is read off `seeding`, a shuffle the server fixes once when registration closes, off the daemon's seed and the tournament's age, so a `--seed` daemon seeds the same way every run and anyone holding the seeding and the rounds recomputes the pairing. Generic over the entrant's name (`K`), so the protocol carries `swiss::Round<PublicKey>` and the client computes `TournamentInfo::standings()` itself — a disputed standing is a recomputation, which is what the design asked for. The protocol re-exports the crate as `swiss`. Rejected: putting the pairing in the protocol crate (it is the wire and nothing else) or in the server (the client could not check it).
   - **`TournamentState` grew `Playing { round }` and `Finished`**, and `TournamentInfo` the `seeding` and the `rounds`. Four messages: `BeginRound` (the first closes registration at two entrants or more and fixes the seeding; each needs the round before complete), `FinishTournament` (after a complete round: the standings are final), `Sit` (the seat at this round's table — a seek, answered `Queued` until the opponent sits, stood up from with `CancelSeek`, then `MatchJoined`; the game is dealt from the two *registered* lists on the pairing's sides, nothing brought) and `RecordResult` (the organizer's, for a table of the current round with no result and no game under way — a no-show, or what the players agreed: 2.5.8, 3.2.1; a player waiting there is stood up and told). The game's end writes the table's result through `start_match`'s `TableRef` — a win either way, a stall a tie — and the game is **not on the ladder**: a tournament's standings are its own rating. **`Tournament` is now pushed unasked** to every attached entrant and the organizer when anything about it changes (`Registry::attached` holds every connection's key and channel; the requester is left out, since its answer is the same message), so a player waiting on the page sees the pairing when it is posted and the organizer sees the entrants arrive and the tables finish.
   - **The pages are the same pages.** In the rounds the standings stand where the entrants did, each table is a line with its names and result, the bye is named, a paired key has "Sit at your table" (the terminal's row, the desktop's button) and "waiting for your opponent" with a Stand up while seated, and the organizer alone is drawn the next round (once every table has a result), the end, and a result per table nobody played — read off `netrunner_client::tournament` (`round_line`, `table_line`, `bye_line`, `standing_row`, `my_table`) so both clients say the same thing; the server is what refuses anyone else. **Back from a tournament game the page is the tournament's**, not the Server page (`OnlineForm::reopen`, `OnlineScreen::returned`): the result is there and the next round will be. A push about another tournament while the Hold form is up leaves the form alone (a new id opens its page; a listed one is put in the list).
   - Tests: the crate's own (scoring; SoS and ESoS and the seeding's tiebreak; round 1 from the seeding with the last as the bye and the later bye to the lowest without one; a rematch avoided by the search and allowed when nothing else is left; sides balanced; the wire); the server's over real sockets (three entrants: the first round closes registration and pairs one table with a bye; a late registration refused; the bye has no table; the two sit and are seated on the pairing's sides with the decks they registered; the next round, a recorded result and the end refused while the game runs; a surrender writes the result and every entrant on is told past their game's end; round 2's bye is the loser; a no-show recorded as a tie; the standings; a restarted daemon holds the rounds); the desktop model's (the paired key offered the seat and the bye not, the seat a seek stood up from, the organizer's offers only when they apply, a push leaving the form alone, reopen keeping the page); and both clients' end-to-end over a daemon (organizer and entrant register, round 1 begun and pushed, both sit and both boards open on the pairing's sides, the concession is the table's result and both come back to the page with the standings drawn and the next round offered, round 2 recorded as a tie, the end with its final standings).
   - **Left for later stages:** the round clock and the end-of-round rule (1.1.5.3), a drop mid-event (built below), an intentional draw offered by the players (today the organizer records it), the seed commit-reveal (c), the cut (d), double-sided Swiss and asynchronous rounds. Appendix II's rounds-by-attendance is not shown, because the table was not at hand to copy.

   **Stage (b), the leftovers — a drop mid-event** (`feat/tournament-drop`, 9 October 2026). The policies' player who leaves is not erased: `Unregister` in the rounds is a drop, and the entry and every result stay (`TournamentInfo::dropped`, kept in the file) — the standings keep the row, marked "dropped", and the opponents' Strength of Schedule still counts the games against it, as Cobra's does — while `swiss::pair`, told who has dropped, leaves them out of every later table and the bye (the bye goes to the lowest *remaining* without one). A table of the current round the dropper has not played is forfeit: the opponent takes the win, and whoever is waiting at it is stood up and told which of the two it was. A drop with a game under way is refused — the board's Surrender is the way out of a game, and it writes the loss through the table — so no game outlives its player's entry, and a round with fewer than two players left is refused with "end the tournament". In both clients the same Withdraw message is a "Drop from the tournament" row or button on a tournament in its rounds, for an entrant who has not dropped (`tournament::may_drop`), the standing line says the results stand, and the round line says the round is played without them. Rejected: ending the running game from the drop (a second way to end a game, when the board has one), and taking the drop out of the standings (its opponents' tiebreakers would move). Tests: the crate's (a drop neither paired nor the bye, its row and its opponent's SoS kept, two of three leaving no bye), the server's over real sockets (a waiting opponent handed the table and stood up, "dropped already", round 2 with no bye and the drop at no table, a drop refused mid-game and taken after the concession, "fewer than two", the drops in the final standings and on a restarted daemon), the desktop model's (the drop offered to the paired and not after it), and the words'.

   **Stage (b), the leftovers — the players' intentional draw** (`feat/tournament-draw`, 9 October 2026). The policies' intentional draw (2.5.8) is agreed between the two players, and until now only the organizer could record one. `OfferDraw` is one player's offer at their table of the current round: published to the table (`TournamentInfo::draw_offers`, `DrawOffer { table, by }`, kept in the file) and standing until it is answered — the opponent's offer back is the agreement, the table's result a tie, and a player seated and waiting there stood up and told — or until it lapses: when the game starts (a game under way is the table's answer, and takes no offer), when a result is recorded or forfeit, and with the round. A second offer from the same key is refused, and so is one from a key with no table or at a table with a result. In both clients the table's line names who has offered, the player's own round line says an offer stands or that the opponent's is waiting for one back, and "Offer a draw" is a row or a button for a paired key with no offer standing (`tournament::may_offer_draw`, `has_offered_draw`, `draw_offers_at`). The policies' five-minute window is left to the round clock, which is not built. Rejected: an offer carried on `swiss::Table` (the pure crate is scoring and pairing, and an offer is a passing state of one round), and a withdrawal of an offer (it lapses with the game, which is what a player who changes their mind sits down to). Tests: the server's over real sockets (an offer before any round refused; one offer not a draw and pushed; the same key refused twice; the opponent's back a tie with the waiting Corp stood up; an offer at a played table refused; an offer lapsing when the game starts and refused while it is under way), the desktop model's (offered once, not with one standing, never by the bye), and the words'.

   **Stage (b), the leftovers — the round clock and the end-of-round rule** (`feat/tournament-round-clock`, 9 October 2026). A round begun now carries its clock (`TournamentInfo::clock`, `RoundClock { began_at, seconds }`): forty minutes, single-sided Swiss's (1.1.5.2), the daemon's `--round-minutes`. **The clock is the server's rule and the client's countdown:** both pages say what is left to the minute off their own clock (`tournament::clock_line`; the desktop page redraws itself every half minute for it) and that time is called, and the server is what acts on it — a seat at a table of the round is refused once time is called ("the organizer records the table"), an intentional draw is offered only within the policies' five minutes of the round starting (2.5.8, the whole of a shorter round), and a game under way is told (`ServerMessage::TimeCalled { turn }`, a line in both boards' logs) and runs the end-of-round rule (1.1.5.3) inside `MatchSession`: the turn in play is finished, the other side takes one more — the turn counter moving twice past the one time fell in, checked after every applied action whichever arm applied it — and then more agenda points wins, even is a tie. **A tie is the one end with no winner:** `GameEnded { winner: Option<Side>, reason: GameEndReason::TimeCalled }`, a fourth transport outcome beside surrender, disconnect and the decision clock, so `MatchMessage::Ended` carries `Option<Side>`, the terminal's box says "A tie", the desktop's end panel says "A tie" with a closing sound, the receipt has no winner, the table takes a `Tie` and nothing is rated. Each match task sleeps against the round's end rather than a tournament task waking every game, because the game already has the one `select!` every clock it answers to runs in. Rejected: ending every game at the call (the policies let the turns finish), and the organizer calling time by hand (the clock is the policies' and a button would be a second rule). Tests: the session's (two random walkers in the chairs under a paused clock: both told the turn time fell in, the game ending exactly two turns past it on agenda points), the server's over real sockets (a two-second round: the clock on the info, both seats told, a seat and a draw refused once time is called, the table recorded), and the words' (the countdown to the minute, under a minute, called, none outside a round). Left: the five-minute reconnect grace of 1.5.6 (the daemon's `--reconnect-grace-secs` is the knob, not yet a tournament's), and Appendix II's rounds by attendance.

   **Stage (c): the seed commit-reveal** (`feat/tournament-seed-commit-reveal`, 9 October 2026). A tournament game is no longer dealt from the daemon's seed: the shuffle is the server's and both players', and none of the three chooses it.
   - **The commitment comes first.** Beginning a round makes a secret for each table and publishes only its hash (`TournamentInfo::seeds`, `statements::TableSeed { round, table, commitment, reveal }`, the hash `statements::seed_commitment` under its own domain), kept with the tournament in its file. **Each seat brings a nonce** with its `Sit`, made by the client's driver as the registration salt is, and names the commitment it read; the server refuses a commitment that is not the table's ("read the tournament again") and a nonce that is not 1 to 64 letters and digits, since the seed's hash separates its parts with newlines. When the second player sits the game is seeded with `statements::table_seed(secret, corp nonce, runner nonce)` — the first eight bytes of a SHA-256 — and the nonces are kept beside the secret.
   - **The reveal is the result.** A table's secret and nonces are published the moment it has a result — a game's end, the organizer's record, a forfeit, an agreed draw — with no nonces for a table nobody played (`SeedReveal`); `TableSeed::check` says whether the secret is the one committed to and what seed it makes. The server's signed **receipt carries the table's seed** (`Receipt::table`), so the record it hashes can be checked to have been dealt from it: its header's `seed` is the one the three made.
   - **The player's signature binds the nonce.** A table seat's `SeatStatement` names the table, the commitment and the nonce (`statements::TableSeat`), and its deck hash is the one the seat *registered*, under the registration's salt. The client machine signs it only when all three are the ones it sat with and the hash is the registered one for its side — the driver reads the registration back from `registrations.jsonl` (`identity::registration_for`) and hands it to `Connection::sit`, the one way a `Sit` is sent now. **Before this stage a tournament seat never signed at all:** the machine checked the deck against the chair a seek brought, and a `Sit` brings none.
   - **What the pages say:** under the standings, whether every secret the server revealed is the one it committed to and how many games' seeds can be checked, or which tables' do not (`tournament::seeds_line`); a seat is taken with the page's commitment (`tournament::my_seed_commitment`).
   - Rejected: the nonce inside the seat statement alone, as the design first said — the statement is signed after `MatchJoined`, when the game is already dealt, so the nonce has to arrive with the `Sit`; the statement then binds it. And the reveal as a message of its own: a result already publishes the tournament, and the reveal is a fact about the table.
   - Tests: the statements' (the commitment holds only for its secret, the seed moves with each part and with the chairs swapped, a reveal with no game, the nonce's alphabet); the machine's (a table seat signed only for its commitment, nonce and registered deck, never through `submit`); the server's over real sockets (a commitment per table and no secret before a result; another table's commitment and a spaced nonce refused; the played table's reveal checking, each chair's own nonce in it, the unplayed table's with none; the kept receipt signed and naming the seed, the record's hash its own and its header's seed the derived one); the terminal's end-to-end round, through the real driver (the reveal checking, and both seats' signed statements in the daemon's receipt naming the table's commitment); the words'.
   - **Left:** a player's record and receipt for a tournament game sent to the player (the server keeps both, and a player cannot yet fetch them to replay their draws themselves), and the page checking the player's own nonce against the reveal (the client does not keep the nonce past the game).
